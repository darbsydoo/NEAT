Using NEAT
Stars 101

NEAT trains you to recognize constellations as they are likely to appear under your own viewing conditions. Playing with the magnitude slider while you look at the stars is a critical part of this: it simulates how the sky looks under different levels of light pollution, and lets you practice under your normal viewing conditions.

This is not a program to hold up while looking at the night sky; for that there are good programs like Star Walk 2. NEAT is a training program for when you are back home under a roof and want to practice, so that out under the sky you can recognize the constellations and know the stars on your own.

Getting started

star choice picks which constellations to practice. Choose a group; its constellations turn gold in the list. When you are just starting, it is best to choose a group with fewer constellations, or constellations you already recognize.

At the bottom of the star choice list, Special stars opens guided tours: sets of stars with something in common, shown one at a time with a short explanation. Press next to move on.

star training is the main function of the program. It takes you through the constellations you chose in star choice, in random order. The number beside it (1x to 4x) sets how many times each one comes up, each time at a new tilt.

The magnitude (visibility) slider next to the star training button sets the visibility at which the sky first appears, in training and when you go to a constellation yourself. It is a reverse scale, so lower numbers mean seeing only the brighter stars. If you haven't worked out your local visibility, leave it around 4.5 to 5.0. That isn't perfect visibility, but most of the stars you need are there.

Latitude, which you should have set when the program first opened, sets how the constellations are rotated, and dims the part of the sky that never rises where you are.

In training

  1. Look first. The stars fade up from the brightest. Try to name the constellation before you reveal it.
  2. If it isn't clear which constellation it is, pan around with your fingers to get your bearings, and play with the magnitude slider to focus on just the brightest stars.
  3. When you are ready to see the constellation, press reveal. After that, press continue to learn more and more about the constellation, its stars and its neighbors.
  4. After everything has been shown, the button reads next, which brings you to the next constellation.

Looking around

Drag with one finger to move across the sky. Pinch to zoom. Twist two fingers to turn the view. The N button points to north; tap it to turn north back to the top.

The reset button brings you back to your initial view of the constellation.

Tap a star for its details. In training this works only after the constellation has been revealed.

Home brings you back to the home page.

Stars 101

The Stars 101 part of the guide gives an overview of how stars evolve and what the naming means. We recommend going back to it a few times as you get more familiar with the types of stars. It also makes clear how much a star's mass (M☉) determines its destiny.

Life cycle of stars

The letters began as an alphabetical ranking by how strong a star's hydrogen lines were, with A strongest. They were later reordered by temperature. O and B jumped to the front because they're the hottest of all, hot enough to strip hydrogen's electrons, which weakens its lines.

Putting it together

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram plots stars by temperature (the letters along the bottom) and brightness. Main-sequence stars, giants, supergiants and white dwarfs fall into separate bands.
